Most budgeting advice starts with "take your monthly income". That works for a salary. It doesn't work for a student in Delhi who earns from tutoring when there are students to tutor, or a part-timer in Melbourne whose shifts change every week.

It also doesn't fit a first job very well. Someone starting work in Mexico City might get paid twice a month. An apprentice in Berlin has a fixed but small amount, and rent takes most of it.

When the money is small, the margin for error is small too. You don't need a full plan. You need to know what is left, and how much of it you can spend today.

## Start with one number

The overall budget is one limit for everything you spend. The free plan includes it, plus up to 2 category budgets.

For a small income, one overall number is usually enough to start. The student in Delhi might set ₹8,000 for the month. The apprentice in Berlin, after rent, might set €400.

Then log what you spend. Type it, say it, or scan the receipt. The free plan gives you 30 captures every 30 days, shared between voice entries, receipt scans and installment screenshots, and typing is unlimited.

## A day at a time

Open a budget and it tells you what that leaves per day: "€14 a day for the 9 days left", worked out from what is left and the days remaining.

That number is more useful than the monthly limit when money is tight. It moves with you. Spend a lot on Monday and Tuesday's number goes down. Have a quiet week and it goes up.

The budget also looks at your pace. If you keep spending the way you have so far this month, it says so before the money runs out: "Still solvent, but on this pace the period ends €40 short."

## A period that matches how you get paid

A budget doesn't have to be monthly. It can run by the day, the week, the month or the year.

The part-timer in Melbourne gets paid weekly, so a weekly budget of A$250 fits better than a monthly one that hides a bad week. Someone paid twice a month can still use a monthly budget and move the month's start in Settings, to any day from 1 to 28, so the month begins with a paycheck instead of the calendar.

## Carrying over what's left

Turn on "Carry over what's left" on a budget and whatever you didn't spend this period is added to the next one. That is a good fit for uneven income: a light week builds a cushion for a heavy one.

It works the other way too. If you went over, the next period starts smaller by that amount, so overspending doesn't quietly disappear.

## Logging the money that comes in

Log income as it arrives. When the tutoring payment lands, add it as income to the account it went into. You see what came in and what went out side by side, without pretending you know next month's total.

If part of your income does repeat, like a monthly stipend or allowance, make it a repeating income entry. Outlayr adds it with its scheduled date the next time you open the app after it comes due.

## Two categories that matter

The 2 free category budgets are worth spending on the things that blow up a small budget. For many students that is food delivery and going out. For someone in a first job it might be transport and lunch.

The Mexico City first jobber might give eating out its own MX$1,500 a month, inside the overall budget. When that one runs low, the rest of the month is still visible.
